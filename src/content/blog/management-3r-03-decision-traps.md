上一篇談了管理者的工作長什麼樣：在 9 分鐘的碎片裡做判斷。這一篇處理那個判斷本身——

> **為什麼聰明人會做出災難性的決策——而且事後覺得自己當時很理性？**

## 你的大腦有兩套系統

Daniel Kahneman 與 Amos Tversky 描繪的雙系統，是理解這件事的地基：

| | 系統一（快思） | 系統二（慢想） |
|---|---|---|
| 運作方式 | 自動、聯想、毫不費力 | 刻意、邏輯、費力 |
| 擅長 | 模式辨識、熟悉情境的直覺 | 計算、比較、抽象推理 |
| 弱點 | 用「感覺對」取代「真的對」 | 懶惰——能不上工就不上工 |

致命之處在分工方式：**多數判斷由系統一先給答案，系統二常常只是事後合理化**。你的理性是被雇來寫辯護狀的律師，不是法官。「事後覺得自己很理性」的機制就在這裡。

那直覺什麼時候可信？Kahneman 與直覺研究者 Gary Klein 吵了多年後達成的共識是三個條件：**環境有規律＋長期練習＋回饋即時**——消防員讀火場、棋手讀棋局，三者齊備。商業決策（併購、進入新市場）幾乎都不滿足：環境不規律、樣本少、回饋要好幾年。在這些場合，「我感覺這個案子行」不是資訊，是警訊。

## 五個每天在燒錢的偏誤

1. **錨定**：第一個數字綁架後續估計。談判先開價、預算沿用去年數字加減 5%，都是被錨定。
2. **確認偏誤**：先有結論，再蒐集支持證據。愛上一個點子後，你看到的全是市場需求的「證據」。
3. **沉沒成本**：「都投了 500 萬，現在停損太可惜」——那 500 萬無論如何都回不來，唯一該問的是「從今天起再投的錢值不值」。
4. **過度自信／規劃謬誤**：估三個月的專案做了九個月。人對「自己這一次」的估計，永遠樂觀於統計基準率。
5. **損失趨避**：賠 100 的痛苦約是賺 100 快樂的兩倍。這讓人抱住賠錢的部位不放，不敢砍掉失敗的產品線。

外加一個管理場景特有的：**動機性偏誤**——提案團隊自己也想要案子過（獎金、地盤、面子），他們給你的分析在源頭就歪了。

## 解方：不靠意志力，靠流程

這篇文章最重要的一句話：**知道偏誤存在，幾乎不能防止偏誤。** 偏誤是系統一的自動輸出，你察覺不到它發生——就像你可以學會辨識視錯覺，但你還是「看到」錯的長度。

所以防線要建在流程裡，而不是腦子裡：

1. **決策品質檢查清單**（Kahneman–Lovallo–Sibony）：審別人的提案時逐項問——提案團隊有沒有動機性推理？是不是只給一個選項？比較過基準率了嗎（同類案子的歷史成功率）？最壞情境認真算過嗎？
2. **事前驗屍（premortem，Gary Klein）**：拍板前，全員假設「一年後這個案子已經失敗」，各自寫下失敗原因。把「唱反調」變成作業，就繞過了不敢潑冷水的社交壓力——研究顯示這能多挖出約三成的風險因子。
3. **指定唱反調者**：制度化的反對意見，讓挑戰提案不需要勇氣。
4. **決策日誌**：記下當時的假設與理由，覆盤時檢討流程、不檢討人——這也讓你日後能分辨「決策錯了」與「運氣不好」。

值得注意：那份檢查清單只能用來審**別人的**提案——因為你看不到自己的系統一。這也是為什麼「一個人反省」的效果遠不如「一群懂偏誤語言的人互相審查」。決策品質不取決於聰明，取決於流程設計。

## 動手試試

挑一個你近期做過的重要決定（投資一檔標的、接或辭一份工作都行），穿越回決策前夕做一次 premortem：假設「一年後它被證明是錯的」，寫下五個最可能的失敗原因——要具體，不是「市場不好」，是「這檔股票的毛利假設建立在單一大客戶不流失上」。然後逐條檢查：當初決策時，這條評估過嗎？

## 自我檢核三問

1. 你上一個重要決定，有沒有寫下當時的預期？如果沒有，你要怎麼知道自己是判斷力好還是運氣好？
2. 你的團隊（或家庭）裡，誰敢對你的提案潑冷水？如果沒有這個人，你打算怎麼制度化地製造一個？
3. 手上有沒有一件「因為已經投入很多」而繼續的事？用增量重新算一次：從今天起再投入的，值得嗎？

The last piece looked at what a manager's day is really like: judgement calls made inside nine-minute fragments. This piece is about the judgement itself —

> **Why do smart people make disastrous decisions — and feel perfectly rational while making them?**

## Your brain runs two systems

The two-system picture drawn by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky is the foundation:

| | System 1 (fast) | System 2 (slow) |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Automatic, associative, effortless | Deliberate, logical, effortful |
| Good at | Pattern recognition, intuition in familiar settings | Calculation, comparison, abstraction |
| Weakness | Substitutes "feels right" for "is right" | Lazy — stays off duty whenever it can |

The fatal part is the division of labour: **most judgements are answered first by System 1, and System 2 often merely rationalizes afterwards.** Your reason is a lawyer hired to write the defence, not a judge. That is the mechanism behind "I felt perfectly rational at the time."

So when can intuition be trusted? After years of debate, Kahneman and intuition researcher Gary Klein agreed on three conditions: **a regular environment, long practice, and immediate feedback** — firefighters reading a burning building, chess players reading a position. Business decisions (acquisitions, new markets) almost never qualify: irregular environments, few samples, feedback years away. There, "I have a good feeling about this deal" is not information. It is a warning light.

## Five biases that burn money daily

1. **Anchoring** — the first number hijacks every later estimate: opening offers, last year's budget ±5%.
2. **Confirmation bias** — conclusion first, evidence after. Fall in love with an idea and all you see is "proof" of demand.
3. **Sunk cost** — "we've already put in five million, quitting now would waste it." That money is gone either way; the only question is whether the *next* dollar is worth investing.
4. **Overconfidence / the planning fallacy** — the three-month project that takes nine. Estimates about "our case, this time" are always rosier than the base rate.
5. **Loss aversion** — losing 100 hurts about twice as much as gaining 100 feels good. Hence holding losing positions and refusing to kill failing product lines.

Plus one specific to management: **motivated reasoning** — the proposing team wants the project approved (bonus, turf, face), so the analysis arrives pre-bent.

## The fix: process, not willpower

The most important sentence in this article: **knowing about biases barely prevents them.** Bias is System 1's automatic output; you cannot feel it happening — just as you can learn about an optical illusion and still see the wrong length.

So the defences go into process, not into heads:

1. **A decision-quality checklist** (Kahneman–Lovallo–Sibony) for reviewing other people's proposals: any motivated reasoning? Only one option presented? Was the base rate consulted? Was the worst case genuinely priced?
2. **The premortem** (Gary Klein): before signing off, everyone assumes "it is one year later and this project has failed" and writes down why. Turning dissent into homework routes around the social pressure not to be negative — and surfaces roughly a third more risk factors.
3. **A designated devil's advocate** — institutionalized opposition, so challenging a proposal requires no courage.
4. **A decision journal** — record the assumptions and reasoning at the time; review the process, not the person. It is also the only way to later tell bad decisions from bad luck.

Note the fine print: the checklist works only on *other people's* proposals — you cannot see your own System 1. Which is why one person reflecting is worth far less than a group that shares the language of bias and reviews each other. Decision quality is not a function of intelligence; it is a function of process design.

## Try it

Take one significant decision you made recently — an investment, taking or leaving a job. Travel back to the eve of the decision and run a proper premortem: assume "one year on, it has proved wrong," and write five concrete reasons why. Concrete means not "the market turned bad" but "the margin assumption depended on one major customer staying." Then check each item: did the original decision ever price it in?

## Three questions for yourself

1. Did you write down your expectations before your last big decision? If not, how will you ever distinguish judgement from luck?
2. Who in your team (or family) dares to pour cold water on your proposals? If nobody, how will you institutionalize one?
3. Is there something you continue only because you have already invested so much? Re-run the numbers incrementally: from today onward, is the next dollar worth it?
