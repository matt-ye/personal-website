先把概念說清楚，生活中說的「利率」其實有好幾層：

央行設定最上游的「政策利率」，這個數字往下傳，最終影響你的房貸月繳、定存利息、甚至你持有股票的價值。

台灣的央行是**中華民國中央銀行**（CBC），美國是**聯邦準備理事會**（Fed，Federal Reserve）。因為台灣對美國的出口依賴度很高，美國升降息常常也會影響台灣的金融市場。

## 央行為什麼要動利率？

<div class="callout">
  <strong>升息</strong>（提高政策利率）：借錢變貴 → 消費和投資放緩 → 壓制通膨<br>
  <strong>降息</strong>（降低政策利率）：借錢變便宜 → 刺激消費和投資 → 提振景氣
</div>

你們做了二十幾年生意，一定有這種感受：景氣好的時候，大家搶著進貨、擴店，這時通膨容易起來；景氣差的時候，大家縮手觀望，企業不敢貸款擴張。

央行就是靠調整「借錢的成本」來影響這個節奏——有點像是幫車子踩油門或煞車。

---

## 升息為什麼讓股市跌？

這是最反直覺的部分，很多人搞不清楚。讓我用一個簡單的問題來解釋：

> **一家公司預計 5 年後會賺 100 萬，今天值多少錢？**

這叫做**折現**（Discounting）——把未來的錢換算成現在的價值。利率越高，未來的錢折算回今天就越少：

<div class="formula">
  折現率 2%（低利率）：100 萬 ÷ 1.02⁵ ≈ <strong>90.6 萬</strong><br>
  折現率 5%（中等利率）：100 萬 ÷ 1.05⁵ ≈ <strong>78.4 萬</strong><br>
  折現率 10%（高利率）：100 萬 ÷ 1.10⁵ ≈ <strong style="color:#dc2626">62.1 萬</strong>
</div>

同樣是「5 年後的 100 萬」，利率從 2% 升到 10%，今天的價值少了將近三成。

**股票的價格，本質上就是市場對一家公司「所有未來獲利折現加總」的估計。** 利率一升，這個折現值就縮水，股價就跌。

這也解釋了為什麼**成長型科技股**在升息時跌最慘：它們的獲利大多在很遠的未來，折現率一升，損失比傳統產業更大。台積電或台灣的科技廠商，獲利穩定且當期，相對抗跌。

## 升息怎麼影響房地產？

這個比較直接。你們如果有貸款買房子或商用不動產，一定感受過：

<div class="formula">
  房貸 1,000 萬，30 年期<br>
  利率 2%：月繳約 <strong>36,965 元</strong><br>
  利率 3%：月繳約 <strong>42,160 元</strong>（多 5,000 元 / 月）<br>
  利率 4%：月繳約 <strong style="color:#dc2626">47,742 元</strong>（多 10,777 元 / 月）
</div>

同樣一筆貸款，利率差 2%，每個月多繳超過一萬。這會直接壓縮潛在買家的負擔能力，讓市場買氣降溫，進而對房價形成壓力。

<p class="source-note">本文的數字都是<strong>示範用的計算</strong>，不是取自任何統計來源——折現值用 現值 = 未來值 ÷ (1+r)ⁿ，房貸月付用本息平均攤還公式（1,000 萬、30 年、年利率 2%／3%／4%）算出。你可以自己代入不同數字驗算。實際房貸利率請以各銀行公告為準。</p>

## 一個很重要的提醒

學了這些，你可能會想：「那我就等降息再買股票，升息就賣掉？」

這個邏輯聽起來很合理，但實際上幾乎行不通，原因有三：

**1. 市場早就定價了**
當央行宣布升息，機構投資人早在幾個月前就預判到了，股價已經先跌過一輪。等你看到新聞再行動，往往已經太慢。

**2. 升息不一定股市就跌**
2022 年美國激進升息，股市大跌；但歷史上也有很多升息週期，股市反而上漲——因為升息通常伴隨著強勁的經濟成長。

**3. 你的持有期間比升降息週期長**
一個升息週期通常 1-2 年，但長期投資者持有的是 10-20 年。在這個尺度下，單次的升降息影響相對有限。

<div class="callout">
  <strong>正確的心態是：</strong>理解「為什麼市場在反應」，而不是試圖預測「市場會怎麼反應」。<br><br>
  當市場因升息大跌，你不會恐慌性賣出——因為你知道這是正常的折現邏輯在運作，而不是世界末日。
</div>

## There is more than one "interest rate"

First, the concept. What we casually call "the interest rate" is really several layers:

The central bank sets the policy rate at the top of the chain. That number travels downstream and eventually shapes your monthly mortgage payment, the interest on your deposits, and even the value of the shares you hold.

Taiwan's central bank is the **Central Bank of the Republic of China (CBC)**; in the US it is the **Federal Reserve (the Fed)**. Because Taiwan's economy leans heavily on exports to the US, American rate moves often ripple through Taiwanese markets too.

---

## Why does the central bank move rates?

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Raising rates</strong> (a higher policy rate): borrowing gets expensive → spending and investment slow → inflation is held down<br>
  <strong>Cutting rates</strong> (a lower policy rate): borrowing gets cheap → spending and investment are stimulated → the economy is lifted
</div>

Twenty-odd years in business will have given you the feel for this: when times are good, everyone rushes to stock up and open new stores, and that is when inflation tends to build; when times are bad, everyone pulls back and waits, and businesses won't borrow to expand.

The central bank steers that rhythm by adjusting the cost of borrowing — rather like using the accelerator and the brake.

---

## Why do rate hikes push stocks down?

This is the counter-intuitive part, and a lot of people never get it straight. Let me explain with one simple question:

> **A company is expected to earn NT$1,000,000 five years from now. What is that worth today?**

This is **discounting** — converting future money into present value. The higher the interest rate, the less that future money is worth today:

<div class="formula">
  Discount rate 2% (low): NT$1M ÷ 1.02⁵ ≈ <strong>NT$906,000</strong><br>
  Discount rate 5% (moderate): NT$1M ÷ 1.05⁵ ≈ <strong>NT$784,000</strong><br>
  Discount rate 10% (high): NT$1M ÷ 1.10⁵ ≈ <strong style="color:#dc2626">NT$621,000</strong>
</div>

Same "NT$1,000,000 in five years" — but moving the rate from 2% to 10% wipes out nearly a third of today's value.

**A share price is essentially the market's estimate of all of a company's future profits, discounted back to today.** Raise rates and that discounted value shrinks, so the price falls.

It also explains why **growth-oriented tech stocks** fall hardest during hikes: most of their profit sits far in the future, so a higher discount rate costs them more than it costs traditional industries. TSMC and Taiwan's established tech manufacturers, whose profits are steady and near-term, hold up comparatively well.

## How do rate hikes reach real estate?

This one is more direct. If you've ever borrowed to buy a home or commercial property, you've felt it:

<div class="formula">
  NT$10M mortgage, 30-year term<br>
  At 2%: about <strong>NT$36,965</strong> a month<br>
  At 3%: about <strong>NT$42,160</strong> a month (NT$5,000 more)<br>
  At 4%: about <strong style="color:#dc2626">NT$47,742</strong> a month (NT$10,777 more)
</div>

The same loan, two percentage points apart, costs over ten thousand more every month. That squeezes what buyers can afford, cools demand, and in turn puts pressure on prices.

<p class="source-note">The figures in this article are <strong>worked examples</strong>, not drawn from any statistical source — present values use PV = FV ÷ (1+r)ⁿ, and the monthly payments use the standard amortisation formula (NT$10M, 30 years, annual rates of 2%／3%／4%). Plug in your own numbers and check. For actual mortgage rates, refer to what each bank publishes.</p>

## One important warning

Having learned all this, you might think: "So I'll buy stocks before a cut and sell before a hike?"

The logic sounds reasonable, but in practice it almost never works. Three reasons:

**1. The market has already priced it in**
By the time the central bank announces a hike, institutional investors anticipated it months ago and prices have already taken their fall. Acting when you see the news is usually too late.

**2. Hikes don't reliably mean falling stocks**
The US hiked aggressively in 2022 and markets fell hard — but history also contains plenty of hiking cycles during which stocks rose, because hikes usually accompany strong economic growth.

**3. Your holding period is longer than the cycle**
A hiking cycle typically runs one to two years. A long-term investor holds for ten to twenty. On that scale, any single rate move matters relatively little.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>The right mindset:</strong> understand <em>why</em> the market is reacting, rather than trying to predict <em>how</em> it will react.<br><br>
  When the market drops on a rate hike, you won't panic-sell — because you'll know it's ordinary discounting arithmetic at work, not the end of the world.
</div>

Interest rates are one of the foundations the whole financial system rests on. They connect: inflation (last piece) → central bank policy → the cost of borrowing → stock valuations → property prices.

Understand that chain and you'll never again be bewildered — or unduly frightened — by a "Fed raises rates" headline.
