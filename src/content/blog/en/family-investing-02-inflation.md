Last time I said that money left in a drawer shrinks over time. Now let me spell out why — the reason is called **inflation**.

---

## Start with something you already know

When you first opened the phone shop, twenty-odd years ago, what did a Nokia handset cost? What does an iPhone cost now?

That isn't a clean inflation comparison, of course — the phones themselves are worlds apart. But after this many years in business you already have the feel for it: the same purchase order buys fewer units than it used to, and the same rent cheque covers less than it used to.

That is what inflation looks like from inside daily life.

---

## What inflation is

**Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level.**

It is measured with the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** — the government regularly tracks the prices of a basket of goods (food, rent, transport, medical care, and so on) and reports the change as a percentage.

## The rule of 72: how fast your money halves

<div class="callout">
  <span class="callout-number">72 ÷ inflation rate = years until purchasing power halves</span>
  2% inflation → half the purchasing power in <strong>36 years</strong><br>
  3% inflation → half the purchasing power in <strong>24 years</strong>
</div>

Put concretely: the NT$1,000,000 you save today will buy about NT$500,000 worth of things by the time you're 85 — even though the number on the statement never changed.

---

## Where does inflation come from?

There are many causes. Three are most common:

**1. Demand-pull**
Everyone has money and competes for the same amount of goods, so prices rise. This is common when the economy is running hot.

**2. Cost-push**
Raw materials, energy, and labour get more expensive, and producers pass the cost on to consumers. You've felt this directly — when the wholesale price goes up, the shelf price has to follow.

**3. Money supply growth**
The government prints too much currency, so each note buys less. Governments worldwide handed out large amounts of relief money during COVID, and the inflation that followed had this ingredient in it.

## What inflation does to savings

Suppose the bank's time-deposit rate is 1.5% and inflation is 2.5%:

<div class="formula">
  real return = nominal rate − inflation rate<br>
  &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;= 1.5% − 2.5%<br>
  &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;= <strong class="num-danger">−1%</strong>
</div>

You earned 1.5%, but your purchasing power fell by 2.5%. The number on the statement grows while what it can buy shrinks. That is why "keep everything in time deposits" is, over the long run, a loss in slow motion.

This isn't to say time deposits are bad — they are liquid and safe, and those are real virtues. But they cannot solve the inflation problem. That gap is what investing is for.

---

## Inflation isn't all bad

Moderate inflation (around 2%) is actually a sign of a healthy economy:

- It means people are willing to spend and businesses are willing to invest
- It eases debt burdens (what you owe shrinks in real terms)
- It gives the central bank room to steer

The genuinely frightening scenario is **deflation** — prices falling continuously. Sounds like a bargain? It isn't: it means nobody dares to spend (they wait for cheaper), business revenue falls, layoffs follow, wages drop, and the cycle feeds itself. Japan fell into exactly this trap in the 1990s and lost the better part of three decades to it.

## So what protects against inflation?

That's a big topic, and the next few pieces go into it. For now, a rough intuition:

| Asset type | Inflation protection | Why |
|----------|:----------:|------|
| Cash / time deposits | <span class="protect-bad">✗ Poor</span> | Real return is often negative |
| Stocks (whole market) | <span class="protect-good">✓ Good</span> | Companies pass costs on and keep up over the long run |
| Real estate | <span class="protect-mid">△ Medium to good</span> | A physical asset, but illiquid |
| Gold | <span class="protect-mixed">？ Unreliable</span> | A short-term haven; over the long run it may not beat inflation |
| Long-dated fixed-rate bonds | <span class="protect-bad">✗ Poor</span> | Lose value when inflation heats up |

A common myth: "inflation is coming, buy gold." Gold is indeed often treated as a short-term haven, but its long-run real return is unstable and well behind owning shares in good companies.

---

## In short

Inflation is not a distant concept that only shows up in the news. It erodes the purchasing power of your savings every single day.

Understanding it isn't meant to alarm you. It's meant to give you a reason to put your money to work — to hold assets that create real value, rather than leaving it lying in an account.
