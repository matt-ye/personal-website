When people come into a sum of money, the first thought is usually "what stock should I buy?"

But before "what to buy" there are four more fundamental questions worth settling. They aren't stock-picking questions — they're the foundation the whole framework rests on. Get these four wrong and even a good stock pick can leave you disappointed with the overall result.

This piece is the entry point to the next three. It lays out all four questions in outline; each gets its own piece afterwards.

**Asset allocation is the precondition for the other three.** You have to know that stocks are 70% before you can discuss how to buy that 70% (entry strategy), what share each individual holding takes (position sizing), and finally what to do when the proportions drift (rebalancing).

## Work it through with NT$1,000,000

Suppose you have NT$1,000,000 to start investing. The four decisions would look like this:

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Scenario: NT$1,000,000, deployed for the first time</strong><br><br>
  <strong>① Asset allocation</strong>: you settle on 70% stocks (NT$700,000), 20% bonds (NT$200,000), 10% cash reserve (NT$100,000)<br><br>
  <strong>② Entry strategy</strong>: rather than buying all NT$700,000 at once, spread it over six months at roughly NT$120,000 a month, cutting the odds of buying the whole lot at a peak<br><br>
  <strong>③ Position sizing</strong>: within the NT$700,000 of stocks, no single company exceeds 10% (NT$70,000); most sits in broad-based ETFs, with individual stocks capped at 30% in total<br><br>
  <strong>④ Rebalancing</strong>: check once at year end, and if the stock weight has drifted more than ±5 percentage points, sell what rose and top up what fell to return to 70/20/10
</div>

Note that not one of those four steps asks "TSMC or Delta Electronics?" Capital-allocation decisions are more fundamental than stock selection — and usually matter more to the final outcome.

---

## Why does allocation matter more than stock picking?

A classic 1990s line of research (Brinson, Hood, and Beebower) analysed the sources of return across a large set of pension funds and concluded that **over 90% of the variability in a portfolio's returns is determined by asset allocation** — not by stock selection, and not by timing.

For an ordinary investor the conclusion is blunter still:

<div class="callout">
  Compared with picking good stocks, <strong>allocating capital to the right categories and holding those proportions with discipline</strong> has a steadier and more predictable effect on long-run wealth.
</div>
