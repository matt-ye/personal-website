Last time we covered ETFs and monthly contributions — sidestepping single-stock risk by holding the market as a whole.

But if you want to understand *why* certain companies are still alive and still profitable ten years later, you need one concept: the **economic moat**.

---

## Where the word comes from

In 1986, Buffett first used the "moat" metaphor in a Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letter:

> "The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society, or how much it will grow, but rather determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage."

By 2007 he had sharpened it into a definition:

> "A truly great business must have an enduring 'moat' that protects excellent returns on invested capital."

<div class="callout">
  <strong>What a moat is</strong>: a <strong>structural advantage</strong> that keeps competitors from eating into a company's profits over the long run.<br><br>
  Like the moat around a medieval castle — the wider and deeper it is, the harder it is to storm. A company without one will see rivals copy its product, take its customers, and grind down its margins in short order.
</div>

---

## Why must a moat be "structural"?

Twenty-odd years running a phone shop in Hsinchu will have shown you the large chains moving in, direct online purchasing taking off, and the handset brands opening their own stores — that is the reality of an industry without a moat.

**Good service and low prices** are not a moat, because a rival can offer better service and lower prices tomorrow.

A moat has to be something **competitors cannot replicate no matter how much they spend**. Morningstar studied thousands of listed companies and organised moats into five sources:

  <div class="moat-card">
    <div class="moat-card-head">
      <div class="moat-icon">🏷️</div>
      <div>
        <div class="moat-title">Intangible assets</div>
        <div class="moat-en">Intangible Assets</div>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="moat-body">
      Brands, patents, government licences. They let a company charge a premium or keep rivals out entirely.
      <div class="moat-example">e.g. Taiwan Mobile's telecom licence, Johnson &amp; Johnson's patented drugs, Coca-Cola's brand premium</div>
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="moat-card">
    <div class="moat-card-head">
      <div class="moat-icon">🔗</div>
      <div>
        <div class="moat-title">Switching costs</div>
        <div class="moat-en">Switching Costs</div>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="moat-body">
      Leaving the product is expensive for the customer — in money, time, risk, or broken habits.
      <div class="moat-example">e.g. a company on SAP's ERP faces six months of disruption to replace it; a settled iPhone user won't move to Android</div>
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="moat-card">
    <div class="moat-card-head">
      <div class="moat-icon">🌐</div>
      <div>
        <div class="moat-title">Network effects</div>
        <div class="moat-en">Network Effects</div>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="moat-body">
      The more users there are, the more the product is worth to each of them. Latecomers find that loop almost impossible to break.
      <div class="moat-example">e.g. Line's group chats (useful only because everyone is on it), Visa's payment network (more merchants → more consumers → more merchants)</div>
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="moat-card">
    <div class="moat-card-head">
      <div class="moat-icon">💲</div>
      <div>
        <div class="moat-title">Cost advantage</div>
        <div class="moat-en">Cost Advantage</div>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="moat-body">
      A structurally lower cost base than rivals, which can be spent on winning share or kept as a fatter margin.
      <div class="moat-example">e.g. Costco's warehouse model, TSMC's economies of scale, Amazon's logistics density</div>
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="moat-card">
    <div class="moat-card-head">
      <div class="moat-icon">📐</div>
      <div>
        <div class="moat-title">Efficient scale</div>
        <div class="moat-en">Efficient Scale</div>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="moat-body">
      The market is small enough that incumbents already meet demand; a new entrant would only hurt everyone, so nobody enters.
      <div class="moat-example">e.g. regional ports, gas pipelines, a wastewater plant serving one area</div>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

*Source: Morningstar Equity Research, and *Why Moats Matter: The Morningstar Approach to Stock Investing* (Pat Dorsey, 2014)*

## Case study: how many moats does TSMC have?

TSMC is Taiwan's most representative moat case. Let's check it against each source:

  <div class="moat-analysis-head">
    <span class="moat-company">TSMC (2330)</span>
    <span class="moat-strength wide">Moat: Wide</span>
  </div>
  <div class="moat-check-list">
    <div class="moat-check-row yes">
      <span class="moat-check-icon">✓</span>
      <div>
        <span class="moat-check-label">Intangible assets</span>
        <span class="moat-check-detail">Forty years of accumulated process know-how, which cannot be bought or reverse-engineered. Below 2nm, only TSMC and Samsung can manufacture at all — and the yield gap between them is enormous.</span>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="moat-check-row yes">
      <span class="moat-check-icon">✓</span>
      <div>
        <span class="moat-check-label">Switching costs</span>
        <span class="moat-check-detail">Apple's A18 chip is designed entirely around TSMC's process. Changing foundry means redesigning the chip, a two-to-three-year job. Customers are locked in deeply.</span>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="moat-check-row partial">
      <span class="moat-check-icon">△</span>
      <div>
        <span class="moat-check-label">Network effects</span>
        <span class="moat-check-detail">There is some ecosystem effect (top engineers cluster there; design-tool vendors optimise for TSMC's process), but nothing as strong as a platform company's.</span>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="moat-check-row yes">
      <span class="moat-check-icon">✓</span>
      <div>
        <span class="moat-check-label">Cost advantage</span>
        <span class="moat-check-detail">US$30–40 billion of capital spending a year, amortised over enormous volume, puts unit costs far below any smaller fab. Gross margin was about 54% in 2023, while Intel's foundry division was still loss-making.</span>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="moat-check-row partial">
      <span class="moat-check-icon">△</span>
      <div>
        <span class="moat-check-label">Efficient scale</span>
        <span class="moat-check-detail">Demand for advanced processes is vast, so this doesn't fit "efficient scale" (which requires a limited market). But the complexity of the process portfolio makes catching up from zero so hard that the effect is similar.</span>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

TSMC's **ROIC (return on invested capital)** was about **24%** in 2023, and has stayed above 20% for years — exactly the financial signature of a moat. A company without structural competitive advantage typically earns something close to its cost of capital (roughly 8–10%).

*Sources: TSMC 2023 annual report; Bloomberg analyst data*

Yes. History is full of large companies whose moats eroded:

  <div class="scenario-card cut">

      <li class="up">High switching costs where switching is also risky (medical, financial software)</li>

      <li class="up">Government licences or regulatory protection (telecom, banking charters)</li>

Pat Dorsey argues in *Why Moats Matter* that **brand is the most frequently misjudged moat**. Many people assume a famous brand is a moat, but a brand only counts as one when it makes consumers willing to pay more. Luxury brands qualify; many consumer-electronics brands do not, because buyers chase the newest features rather than the badge.

## How do you quantify a moat? Look at ROIC

A feeling that "this company is strong" isn't enough. The most direct financial measure is **ROIC (return on invested capital)**:

<div class="formula">
  ROIC = net operating profit after tax (NOPAT) ÷ invested capital<br><br>
  The plain-language version: for every NT$100 the company puts in, how much does it earn in a year?<br><br>
  <strong>ROIC above 15% over time</strong>: there is usually a moat<br>
  <strong>ROIC of 8–15% over time</strong>: a moat may exist, but it isn't wide<br>
  <strong>ROIC below the cost of capital (roughly 8–10%)</strong>: essentially no moat
</div>

## How moat analysis relates to an ETF strategy

You might reasonably ask: "If I only buy ETFs, what is any of this for?"

Two things:

**1. Understanding what an ETF actually holds**
When you buy VOO or 00940, you are in fact holding a great many moated companies. The heaviest weights in the S&P 500 — Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon — all have deep moats without exception. This is why holding a large-cap index ETF for the long run works: the index mechanism automatically retires companies that lose their edge and keeps the ones whose moats endure.

**2. A screen, if you ever add individual stocks**
If you want to hold a few individual names alongside your ETF, "is there a moat, and how wide" is the most important filter. You don't need dozens of financial ratios — the long-run ROIC trend plus the five sources of moat is a sufficient first pass.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>What Buffett actually does:</strong> he admits he often can't tell precisely whether a company has a moat — so his strategy is <strong>to invest only in companies whose moats are obvious enough not to need complicated analysis</strong>. If it takes elaborate reasoning to convince yourself a moat exists, the moat probably wasn't deep enough to begin with.<br><br>
  Source: Berkshire Hathaway 2007 annual shareholder letter
</div>

## In short

- A **moat** is the structural competitive advantage that lets a company sustain above-average profits
- Five sources: intangible assets, switching costs, network effects, cost advantage, efficient scale
- **ROIC above 15% over the long run** is the financial signature of a moat
- Moats aren't permanent — technological change can drain them, and brand is the one most often misjudged
- Even if you only buy ETFs, understanding moats tells you what you actually own

Next we get into the financial statements: **the income statement, the balance sheet, and the cash flow statement — how do you read all three?** That is the last foundational piece for applying moat analysis to an individual company.
