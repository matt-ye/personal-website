Plenty of people have owned stocks for over a decade and still can't say what a stock actually is.

This piece starts from the foundations: why companies exist, how they raise money, what a share represents, and what really happens the moment you press "buy" on an exchange.

---

## Why form a company at all?

When you opened the phone shop you would have registered a business or a company. Why not simply trade under your own name?

There is one core reason: **limited liability**.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Trading personally</strong>: liability is unlimited. If the business fails, creditors can come after your savings, your house, and your future income.<br><br>
  <strong>Forming a company</strong>: liability is limited. Shareholders can lose at most what they put in; personal assets are protected by law, and the company's debts are not your debts.
</div>

This is the most fundamental piece of design in the whole institution of the company — **it lets people take risks and commit capital without fearing that a single failure will ruin them**.

That institution is what made centuries of commercial expansion possible. Without limited liability, nobody would have put up the capital to build TSMC, or Apple, or any large enterprise.

### A company is an independent "legal person"

Once registered, a company acquires **legal personhood** — it is an independent legal entity that can:

- Sign contracts and hold property in its own name
- Borrow, sue, and be sued
- Continue to exist even after the founder leaves or dies

That is fundamentally different from trading in your own name. Your phone shop can keep operating, and be transferred to someone else, even after you retire.

## When you buy a stock, what do you get?

When a company decides to issue stock publicly, it is effectively slicing itself into small pieces of ownership. Each piece is a share.

Say a company has issued 10 million shares in total and you buy 10,000 of them:

<div class="ownership-viz">
  <div class="ownership-bar">
    <div class="ownership-you" style="width: 1%">You 1%</div>
    <div class="ownership-others">The other 99% of shareholders</div>
  </div>
  <p style="margin:0; color: var(--color-text-muted); font-size: 0.82rem;">10,000 shares ÷ 10,000,000 shares = 1% ownership of the company</p>
</div>

Holding that 1% means:

- **A claim on profits**: when the company pays a dividend, you receive 1% of it
- **A residual claim on assets**: if the company is wound up, you get 1% of whatever remains after debts are settled
- **A vote**: at the shareholders' meeting you have 1% of the voice (over board elections and major decisions)

**Note**: owning stock is not the same as running the company day to day. You are an owner, but you have hired management (the CEO, the board) to run it for you. That is the separation of ownership and control.

## Primary market vs. secondary market

This is where a lot of people are confused.

**The primary market**: the company issues shares to outsiders for the first time, in an initial public offering (IPO). This is when investors' money actually lands in the company's account and funds expansion.

**The secondary market**: the stock market you see every day — the Taiwan Stock Exchange (TWSE) here, the NYSE or NASDAQ in the US.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>The key realisation:</strong> when you buy TSMC at NT$800, that NT$800 does not go to TSMC — it goes to whichever shareholder was willing to sell to you. TSMC received its money back at the IPO.<br><br>
  Trading in the secondary market is a transfer of ownership between investors; the company itself is not a party to it.
</div>

This explains why a falling share price doesn't necessarily mean something is wrong with the company — it can simply mean more people want to sell and fewer are willing to take the other side.

## Two kinds of stock: common vs. preferred

Most retail investors own **common stock**: it carries a vote and a share of profits, but ranks last in a liquidation.

**Preferred stock**: usually carries no vote, but its dividend is fixed and paid ahead of common stock, and it ranks ahead of common stock in a liquidation. It behaves rather like a hybrid of a bond and a stock, and ordinary investors encounter it less often.

## The basic vocabulary: EPS and P/E

Knowing "I own part of a company" isn't enough. Two numbers are the minimum for actually assessing a stock:

**EPS (earnings per share)**

<div class="formula">
  EPS = annual net income ÷ total shares outstanding<br><br>
  Example: TSMC's 2024 net income was about NT$1.17 trillion, on roughly 25.9 billion shares<br>
  EPS ≈ 1,170,000,000,000 ÷ 25,900,000,000 ≈ <strong>about NT$45</strong>
</div>

EPS says how much profit is attributable to each share you hold. The higher and the steadier its growth, the more a company is generally worth.

<p class="source-note">Sources for the TSMC figures: the <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1046179/000104617925000004/a4q24e_withguidancexfinal.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Q4 2024 earnings release</a> (filed with the US SEC on Form 6-K) states weighted average shares outstanding of 25,929 million. Full-year net income is the sum of the four quarters, NT$1,173,268 million (<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1046179/000104617924000046/a1q24e_withguidancexfinal.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Q1 225,485</a> + <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1046179/000104617924000083/a2q24e_withguidancexfinal.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Q2 247,845</a> + Q3 325,258 + Q4 374,680), about NT$1.17 trillion, which divided by the share count gives EPS of roughly NT$45.25.<br>The NT$800 price used above is a round number chosen for easy arithmetic, not a quote from any particular date.</p>

**P/E (the price-to-earnings ratio)**

<div class="formula">
  P/E = share price ÷ EPS<br><br>
  Example: a price of NT$800 against EPS of NT$45 → P/E ≈ <strong>17.8×</strong><br>
  Which means: you are paying 17.8 years of earnings to own a piece of this company
</div>

P/E measures how large a multiple of a company's earnings the market is willing to pay.

- High P/E: the market expects strong growth and is pricing it in early (common for growth stocks)
- Low P/E: the market expects steady or declining results and values it conservatively (common in mature industries)

There is no absolute good or bad P/E; it has to be compared within an industry. TSMC at 17× and a software startup at 150× cannot be compared head to head.

## How is buying stock different from betting on a company?

A lot of people see the market as a casino: it goes up, you win; it goes down, you lose.

In the short run the market really is full of speculation and emotion. But over the long run, **stock returns come from the value companies genuinely create** — their products, services, technology, and customers.

You've watched Taiwan's tech industry rise and stumble for over twenty years, and you have a fairly direct sense of which companies create real value. That sense is the most central skill in investing.

## In short

A stock isn't a numbers game. It's a certificate of ownership.

- Companies exist because **limited liability** lets people pool capital and take risks
- Issuing stock is one of three ways to raise money, and it opens funding to far more people
- The stock you buy represents **a slice of ownership in the company**, and a claim on its future profits
- Secondary-market trading is **a transfer of ownership between investors**; the money does not reach the company
- EPS and P/E are the basic valuation vocabulary, and every piece from here on uses them
