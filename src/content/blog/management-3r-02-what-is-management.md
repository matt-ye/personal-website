這是「天時、地利、人和」專欄的第一堂正課。開場先問一個不客氣的問題：

> **如果明天你成為 10 人團隊的主管，你的一天會花在哪裡——而教科書說你「應該」花在哪裡？**

這兩個答案的落差，就是這篇文章要處理的事。我把三個互相打架、又互相成全的經典框架放在同一張桌上：教科書的 POLC、Mintzberg 的碼表實測、Drucker 的效能裁決。

## 教科書的答案：POLC 四功能

管理學教科書把管理者的工作歸納為四個功能：

| 功能 | 在做什麼 | 10 人公司的實例 |
|---|---|---|
| 規劃 Planning | 設定目標、決定路徑 | 訂下「本季營收 300 萬」並拆解到月 |
| 組織 Organizing | 配置人與資源、設計分工 | 決定誰管業務、誰管出貨，會計要不要外包 |
| 領導 Leading | 激勵、溝通、影響他人 | 週會上把壞消息講清楚，讓團隊仍願意衝 |
| 控制 Controlling | 衡量進度、修正偏差 | 看週報發現轉換率掉了，追原因、調做法 |

這個框架的價值在**完整性檢查**：很多新手主管整天在「領導」（開會、救火），完全沒有「控制」（沒有指標、沒有覆盤）。用 POLC 掃一遍自己的工作，漏掉的那格通常就是問題所在。

但 POLC 有一個致命的誤導：它讓人以為管理者的一天，是照這四塊「排課表」過的。

## 碼表的答案：Mintzberg 的跟拍研究

Henry Mintzberg 在 1970 年代做了一件當時沒人做的事：拿著碼表跟拍高階主管。結果推翻了四個流傳甚廣的迷思：

| 迷思 | 碼表下的事實 |
|---|---|
| 管理者是深思熟慮的規劃者 | 平均每件事只花 **9 分鐘**，工作極度零碎、不斷被打斷 |
| 有效的管理者沒有例行雜務 | 大量時間花在儀式性活動——接待、簽核、應酬 |
| 管理者需要的是彙總後的正式報表 | 他們偏愛口頭情報：電話、走廊對話——最新鮮的資訊都不在報表裡 |
| 管理是一門已成熟的科學 | 核心程序（判斷、直覺）仍鎖在大腦裡，靠經驗傳承 |

Mintzberg 進一步把觀察到的行為歸納成十種角色，分三組：**人際**（頭臉人物、領導者、聯絡人）、**資訊**（監控者、傳播者、發言人）、**決策**（創業家、危機處理者、資源分配者、談判者）。

十角色最重要的洞察是：這些角色是一個整體，**不能只挑喜歡的做**。你當上主管的那一刻，就同時領了十頂帽子——差別只在你有沒有意識到自己正在換帽子。技術背景的人最想逃避的通常是頭臉人物與發言人這類「表演型」角色，但逃掉任何一頂帽子，資訊流就斷在你手上。

## Drucker 的裁決：效率 vs 效能

POLC 與十角色描述「管理者在做什麼」，Peter Drucker 回答「怎樣才算做得好」：

> **效率（efficiency）是把事情做對；效能（effectiveness）是做對的事情。**

一個把沒人要看的報表做得又快又漂亮的人，有效率、沒效能。Drucker 觀察數十年高階主管後歸納出八個實踐，我把它壓成三組：

1. **知的紀律**——先問「什麼事必須做？」（不是「我想做什麼」），再問「什麼對組織是對的？」
2. **行的紀律**——寫有期限的行動計畫、為決策與溝通負責、聚焦機會而非問題
3. **群的紀律**——開有生產力的會議、想「我們」而非「我」

他還有一條給所有知識工作者的鐵律：**做好第一優先的事，一次只做一件。**

## 三個框架怎麼合用

- **POLC** 是功能盤點表——檢查你有沒有漏掉哪個面向
- **十角色** 是現實描述——接受工作註定零碎，學會在 9 分鐘的縫隙裡換帽子
- **效能八實踐** 是品質標準——零碎不是藉口，方向錯了再忙都是零分

對想往管理走的人，第一課是這句話：**你的產出＝組織的產出**。你的槓桿不在親自做事，而在決策品質、資訊流通與人才配置。這條原則會貫穿整個專欄。

## 動手試試

連續三天，用 30 分鐘為單位記錄自己的時間，然後替每一格貼兩個標籤：POLC 四功能之一（或「執行」——親自做事不算管理功能），加上 Mintzberg 十角色之一。三天後看一個數字：**「執行」與「管理功能」的比例**。多數第一次做這個練習的人，會被自己「其實沒在管理」的程度嚇到。

## 自我檢核三問

1. 你最近一週的工作，POLC 哪一格完全空白？
2. 十頂帽子裡，你最想逃避哪一頂？逃掉它，什麼資訊流會斷？
3. 你手上有沒有一件「做得很有效率、但其實不必做」的事？

This is the first proper lesson of the Right Time, Right Place, Right People column. Let me open with an impolite question:

> **If you became the manager of a ten-person team tomorrow, where would your day actually go — and where do the textbooks say it should go?**

The gap between those two answers is what this article is about. I put three classic frameworks on the same table: the textbook's POLC, Mintzberg's stopwatch study, and Drucker's verdict on effectiveness. They fight each other, and they complete each other.

## The textbook answer: the four functions (POLC)

Management textbooks reduce the manager's job to four functions:

| Function | What it means | In a ten-person company |
|---|---|---|
| Planning | Set goals, choose the path | Set a quarterly revenue target and break it down by month |
| Organizing | Allocate people and resources | Decide who owns sales, who owns fulfilment, whether to outsource accounting |
| Leading | Motivate, communicate, influence | Deliver bad news at the weekly meeting in a way that keeps people moving |
| Controlling | Measure and correct | Spot a falling conversion rate in the weekly report and chase the cause |

The framework's value is as a **completeness check**: many new managers spend all day "leading" (meetings, firefighting) and none of it "controlling" (no metrics, no reviews). Scan your own week with POLC and the empty box is usually where the trouble is.

But POLC carries a fatal suggestion: that a manager's day is scheduled around these four blocks.

## The stopwatch answer: Mintzberg's field study

In the 1970s Henry Mintzberg did something nobody had done: he followed executives around with a stopwatch. The results demolished four pieces of folklore:

| Folklore | Fact under the stopwatch |
|---|---|
| Managers are reflective planners | The average activity lasts about **nine minutes**; work is fragmented and constantly interrupted |
| Effective managers have no routine duties | Large amounts of time go to ceremony — receptions, sign-offs, social obligations |
| Managers need aggregated formal reports | They prefer oral intelligence: phone calls, corridor talk — the freshest information never sits in reports |
| Management is a mature science | The core processes — judgement, intuition — remain locked in managers' heads |

Mintzberg distilled what he saw into ten roles in three groups: **interpersonal** (figurehead, leader, liaison), **informational** (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson), and **decisional** (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator).

The key insight: the ten roles form a gestalt — **you cannot pick only the ones you like**. The moment you become a manager you are issued all ten hats; the only question is whether you notice yourself switching them. People from technical backgrounds most want to skip the "performing" hats — figurehead, spokesperson — but drop any hat and an information flow breaks at your desk.

## Drucker's verdict: efficiency vs effectiveness

POLC and the ten roles describe what managers do; Peter Drucker answers what doing it *well* means:

> **Efficiency is doing things right; effectiveness is doing the right things.**

Someone who produces a beautiful, fast report that nobody reads is efficient and ineffective. From decades of observing executives, Drucker distilled eight practices; I compress them into three disciplines:

1. **Knowing** — first ask "what needs to be done?" (not "what do I want to do?"), then "what is right for the enterprise?"
2. **Doing** — write action plans with deadlines, take responsibility for decisions and for communication, focus on opportunities rather than problems
3. **Together** — run productive meetings, think and say "we" rather than "I"

And one iron rule for every knowledge worker: **first things first — one thing at a time.**

## Using the three together

- **POLC** is the inventory — check what you are missing
- **The ten roles** are the reality — accept the fragmentation, learn to switch hats inside nine-minute windows
- **The eight practices** are the quality bar — fragmentation is no excuse; if the direction is wrong, busyness scores zero

And for anyone heading into management, lesson one is this sentence: **your output is the output of your organization.** Your leverage is not in doing the work yourself but in decision quality, information flow and the placement of people. That principle runs through this whole column.

## Try it

For three days, log your time in 30-minute blocks. Tag each block twice: with one of the POLC functions (or "execution" — doing the work yourself is not a management function), and with one of Mintzberg's ten roles where it applies. Then look at one number: the ratio of **execution to management functions**. Most people doing this for the first time are startled by how little managing they actually do.

## Three questions for yourself

1. In your past week, which POLC box was completely empty?
2. Of the ten hats, which one do you most want to avoid — and which information flow breaks if you drop it?
3. Is there one thing you are doing very efficiently that does not need to be done at all?
